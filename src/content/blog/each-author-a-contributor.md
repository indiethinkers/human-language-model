> Indie Thinkers is open-source infrastructure for developing human stories. Writers develop essays with editors in public and every contribution becomes part of a shared body of knowledge. The revisions and conversations between writers and editors become learning resources that others can learn from.

## A Simple Question

What if The Atlantic were an open-source project on GitHub?

Most ideas worth exploring often start with simple questions.

Why doesn’t something exist? Why does something work the way it does and how could it be different, better… more sustainable, or more accessible, delightful? If The Atlantic were an open-source project, what problems would that help solve for independent writers?

- Publishing platforms like Substack and Medium provide tools for writers to publish, but they are optimized for the distribution of ideas, not the development of ideas.
- Writers lack meaningful editorial relationships. One of the more helpful aspects of contributing to a major publication is the feedback you receive from human beings.
- Valuable editorial knowledge and 1:1 feedback aren’t as accessible as they could be.
- Independent writers lack communities that can develop and support their work.
- Independent voices benefit from amplifiers in order to reach audiences that would appreciate their work. AI has made prose more abundant. Distribution is a massive barrier.

The process of developing a great piece of writing is completely hidden from public view.

Essays published on Indie Thinkers start out as a pull request on GitHub. Comments from an editor are there for all to see. 1:1 feedback is given during a Live Stream on Substack. The video is recorded, transcribed, and included in the original pull request. A final version is then shipped.

## The Internet Slop Machine

I remember the first time I had split-pea soup as a kid.

The bowl looked like green slop. We were visiting family friends, so I had nowhere to hide. My mom made me sit there and finish every last drop. I was stubborn, but I lost this battle. Decades later, I’ve finally found a professional use for the memory.

The internet is full of slop right now. Unlike the split-pea soup, no one is making us consume it. Platforms are simply very good at placing another bowl in front of us. My fellow Philadelphia Phillies fan [Packy McCormick](https://www.notboring.co/p/make-the-internet-fun-again) captured this well a couple years ago.

> Optimization is slowly killing the internet.<br><br>You can see it most clearly on X, because tweets are the simplest content to make, and because likes are the fastest form of feedback. There, slop has taken over. Slop is the newly popular term for the garbage you see in tweets, LinkedIn posts, YouTube videos, and websites more broadly that is so superficial, mediocre, and banal that the only reason people could possibly create it is to drive some metric they’re optimizing for: likes, views, clicks, whatever.
>
> — [Source](https://www.notboring.co/p/make-the-internet-fun-again)

The reason slop will continue to proliferate is because many of us feel like we have to shout a bit louder in order to stand out now. We have to just publish more, on more platforms, multiple times a day, every single day. Having AI create for you makes this more sustainable and once you start it’s difficult to go back to doing all the work yourself. In many cases, without AI, writers wouldn’t be able to publish nearly as much as they do right now.

Slop existed before ChatGPT launched back in 2022, but AI accelerated its value. Polished prose can now be generated in seconds and long-form writing is exploding much faster than the human attention available to evaluate it.

Indie Thinkers is a clear alternative. A focus on lived experiences, conviction, and spiky points of view. A willingness to say, “This is what I think, and I am willing to be seen thinking it.”

## The Process Is the Product

When one person teaches, two people learn. Most readers encounter only the finished essay. They do not see the questions, wrong turns, disagreements, deletions, or moments of discovery behind it. Indie Thinkers makes that development process visible for writers to learn from.

Each essay begins as a pull request on GitHub. In plain natural language, a pull request starts out as a draft that tracks comments, changes, and revisions.

The process works like this:

1. A writer submits an essay.
2. The essay becomes a pull request.
3. An editor responds with written comments and a live conversation.
4. With the writer’s consent, the conversation is recorded and transcribed.
5. The writer revises the essay.
6. The finished work is published and attributed to its author.
7. The editorial history remains available as a learning resource.

The writer receives sustained attention. The editor helps one person directly. Other writers can observe the decisions and learn from them. A one-to-one relationship becomes one-to-many.

The internet already gives writers feedback, but most of it is thin and reactive. A retweet on Twitter tells you that something traveled but it doesn’t tell you if the idea holds up under objective scrutiny. Metrics reward what attracts attention. They do not necessarily help a writer clarify a thought, strengthen an argument, or recognize what should be cut.

Serious editorial feedback requires time and trust.

The completed essay matters, but showing how a writer got from A to B is a key differentiator for us. When prose can be generated in the blink of an eye, provenance becomes more valuable. Readers can see the thinking, risk, revision, and human judgment behind the work.

The process is the product.

## A Little Help from My Friends

We all need professional help every now and then. God knows I do. What’s funny is we look at people at the top of their game and think they got there by themselves. Grit, determination, and luck in some form. That’s just not true. Show me a genius and I’ll show you their tutors. [Erik Hoel](https://www.theintrinsicperspective.com/p/why-we-stopped-making-einsteins) highlights this in his essay *Why We Stopped Making Einsteins*:

> Einstein had multiple tutors growing up in subjects like mathematics and philosophy, such as his uncle, Jakob Einstein, who taught him algebra. In fact, there was a family tutor of the Einsteins who went by the name Max Talmud (possibly the best name of a tutor *ever*), and it was indeed Max Talmud who introduced the young 12-year-old Albert to geometry, prefacing young Albert’s eventual transformation of our understanding of space and time into something geometric. Maybe we don’t make Einsteins anymore because we don’t make Max Talmuds anymore.
>
> — [Source](https://www.theintrinsicperspective.com/p/why-we-stopped-making-einsteins)

Editors can be modern-day Max Talmuds.

Writing is thinking and is itself a form of feedback for the person with a pen in their hand. An idea can live comfortably in your head because no one in there is asking follow-up questions. Put it on the page and the cracks become immediately obvious.

[Henrik Karlsson](https://www.henrikkarlsson.xyz/p/writing-to-think) describes that transition:

> When I write, I get to observe the transition from this fluid mode of thinking to the rigid. As I type, I’m often in a fluid mode—writing at the speed of thought. I feel confident about what I’m saying. But as soon as I stop, the thoughts solidify, rigid on the page, and, as I read what I’ve written, I see cracks spreading through my ideas. What seemed right in my head fell to pieces on the page.
>
> — [Source](https://www.henrikkarlsson.xyz/p/writing-to-think)

An editor adds another mind to that process. Visibility helps editorial knowledge move from one writer–editor relationship to many others. In David Perell’s *How I Write* podcast, Michael Dean offers a glimpse of what it looks like to make a writer’s process teachable.

## What Open Source Unlocks

I can’t stop thinking about the intersection of code and prose.

In software, open source allows people to inspect how something works, propose changes, study earlier decisions, and build upon what others have made.

GitHub provides a practical place to make editorial knowledge visible, invite different kinds of contribution, and preserve what a community learns. Those needs would remain if the platform disappeared. GitHub is where we live today, but the larger open-source process can survive a change in tools down the line if needed.

Working in public contributes three things:

- **Visible learning:** Other writers can observe genuine editorial decisions. Why an opening changed, why an argument failed, why a paragraph moved, or why a beautiful sentence still had to be cut. This is more useful than abstract advice because it allows emerging writers and educators to study editorial judgment in context.
- **Distributed contribution:** A writer may begin the essay, while editors, artists, educators, technologists, and readers strengthen the larger project. Each contribution is attributable and organized around shared standards, with the writer retaining control of the work itself.
- **A reusable archive:** A finished essay is one artifact. Its revision history, editorial conversation, illustration, citations, and decisions create another. Preserving those materials allows future writers to learn from the work instead of forcing every editorial relationship to begin from zero.

Open-source software has shown how visible work can coordinate contributions across a community. Indie Thinkers borrows that principle while adapting it to the trust, consent, and judgment that prose requires. Openness also creates responsibilities. Obviously not every story, comment, or conversation belongs in public view. Unfinished writing can be vulnerable, and an audience can make honest feedback feel like a performance.

Writers retain copyright in their work. A finished essay can be published under [Creative Commons Attribution 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), while private notes and unfinished drafts remain outside that license unless the writer chooses otherwise.

## A Human Language Model

Before large language models, we had human language models. You know, things like libraries, literary magazines, newspapers, national archives. Useful things.

For Indie Thinkers, “human language model” is a metaphor to describe a growing public archive of human thought and the process used to develop it. It’s preserved stories, ideas, and knowledge. The archive includes essays, revisions, editorial conversations, illustrations, citations, disagreements, and the choices that transformed one version into another. It records what someone thought and how that thinking changed.

A finished essay can make an argument. Its history can teach someone how arguments are built. One writer’s development becomes visible learning. Many contributors add different forms of expertise. The preserved record becomes reusable. Over time, all the work we do as contributors forms a body of knowledge no single writer or editor could produce alone.

Indie Thinkers will be intentional about preserving evidence of a human in the loop.

## The Underdogs of the Internet

Independent writers need tools and people who can recognize promise, help develop it, and introduce the finished work to readers. Discovering hidden gems is a part of our responsibility.

On December 11, 1985, David Letterman interviewed Quincy Jones shortly before *The Color Purple* reached theaters. Jones explained how Oprah Winfrey unexpectedly joined the cast of the film. He had seen “this lady” on *AM Chicago* and thought, “There’s Sofia.” He got her name, requested an audition tape, and it was a wrap. *The Color Purple* received eleven Academy Award nominations. *AM Chicago* became *The Oprah Winfrey Show*.

Q had an incredible ability to discover remarkable talent.

Indie Thinkers can help find what I call the underdogs of the internet. Writers with something meaningful to say who have not yet found the audience capable of recognizing it. Good work still needs somewhere to develop and somewhere to travel.

## The Town Square and the Coffee Shop

Indie Thinkers now exists on digital land that we completely own, but we’ll still congregate in many familiar spaces throughout the interwebs, primarily Twitter and Substack.

Twitter has owned the “internet town square” title for over a decade. News always breaks there first. Companies [announce launches](https://x.com/midjourney/status/1991684484455100477). Movements begin and [memes are born](https://x.com/jordihays/status/1991265404652515800?s=20).

**Twitter is, and will always be, the internet town square.**

When [Google ships a new model](https://x.com/Google/status/1990813116045602942?s=20), they announce it on Twitter. When a [founder wants feedback](https://x.com/catalinmpit/status/1990761240860524853?s=20), they post on Twitter. When journalists need to know what people are talking about, they check Twitter. The platform has become infrastructure.

But the town square is loud. Everyone can see what’s happening. Information moves fast. You bump into people you weren’t expecting. Sometimes you witness something important. Sometimes you just get yelled at.

You can’t think deeply in the town square. Everything you say is a performance. The format pushes you toward quick takes. And there’s this anxiety about who’s watching.

Substack is quite different. **Substack is the coffee shop.**

People are more vulnerable. They share personal struggles. Half-finished ideas. They’re less afraid and more honest. The long-form focus filters for readers who’ll invest time. Twitter and Substack solve opposite problems. Twitter: “I need to know what’s happening right now.” Substack: “I need to think through something that matters.”

## The Hypothesis We’re Testing

Indie Thinkers has moved beyond the idea stage. The [web application](https://www.indiethinkers.com/) has launched. The newsletter has more than 1,100 subscribers. Ten [interviews](https://indiethinkers.substack.com/podcast) with writers have been completed, [nine essays](https://indiethinkers.substack.com/s/guest-lecture) have been published, and the [human language model repository](https://github.com/indiethinkers/human-language-model) is public.

That activity proves people are willing to gather around the idea. It does not yet prove that public editing improves essays, teaches observers, or creates an archive people will use. Those are the questions the next phase must answer.

- Do writers believe the editorial process strengthened their work?
- Would they choose the public process again?
- Can observers explain or apply an editorial decision they encountered in the archive?
- Can people find useful examples without already knowing where to look?
- Does the archive become more useful as its contents grow?
- Do the standards and consent rules protect the candor that serious editing requires?

The proposed flywheel is straightforward:

> A writer contributes → an editor helps develop the work → selected parts of the process become educational material → other writers study it → future contributions improve → valuable human knowledge is published and preserved.

Every arrow needs evidence. If the chain breaks, the model should change.

If it works, one writer will receive the attention needed to make an essay better. One editor’s judgment will travel beyond a single conversation. Another writer will discover that history when facing a similar problem and begin with more knowledge than either of them had before.

An institution could grow around that exchange. A publication that develops independent writers, a workshop that teaches in public, an archive that preserves how human ideas change.

*Each author, a contributor. Every essay, a pull request.*
